\chapter{Introduction}
In which we talk about granaries and justify the importance of the question

Granaries are storage structure for grain, used to protect harvested grain from poor weather and pests.
To prevent spoilage grain needs to kept at a low moisture content; this state may initially be achieved
by harvesting during dry conditions, or by drying harvested grain. Drying sometimes happens at the granary.

It is typical for a granary to service all of the farms in an area, often operating as either a collective or
through commercial agreements.

Grain must be harvested at the correct time of year, and the need for dry weather leads to busy peak periods
for granaries, corresponding with appropriate weather. Deliveries can cluster, as farmers restart harvesting
operations after inclement weather or race to harvest as much as possible before rain.

These high demand periods can lead to significant queues, incurring costs of time and money. Understanding
the likely demands placed on a granary is therefore important to decisions regarding its design and operation.
Planners may wish to invest in equipment to reduce unloading time or add additional service bays; managers may
wish to alter policy regarding opening hours or the order in which trucks are served.

Mathematical models are a useful way of investigating these demands and evaluating different investments and
policies. In this investigation we implement three different models of a granary, and investigate where the models
agree and disagree. We compare to closed form results for queueing systems, and consider where the models likely
fall short of reality.
